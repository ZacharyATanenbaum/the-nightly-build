Nvidia's Coalition Draws the AI Policy Line at Theft, Not Open Weights

Twenty-five companies want Washington to punish covert model extraction without restricting the downloadable weights that support local deployment, competition, and independent security work.

7 min read2026-07-25

Twenty-five firms ask Washington to preserve downloadable models

Nvidia, Microsoft, Meta, IBM, Hugging Face, Mistral and nineteen other companies and organizations signed a July 24 letter urging U.S. policymakers to keep open-weight artificial intelligence models available. The signatories define an open-weight model as one whose learned parameters can be downloaded, inspected, modified and run on infrastructure chosen by the operator. Their requested boundary is specific: punish theft, covert extraction and abuse through targeted legal and commercial rules, while avoiding category-wide limits on lawful access to model weights.1

The intervention lands during a policy argument that has fused three separate questions. One is whether Chinese laboratories obtained capabilities through improper extraction from U.S. systems. Another is whether powerful downloadable models create security risks once their safeguards can be removed. The third is whether customers should remain dependent on a small group of hosted-model vendors. Reuters reported that lawmakers are considering stronger controls after a rogue-model cyberattack, while the administration is weighing measures against Chinese model developers accused of technology theft.2

The letter matters because it tries to stop those questions from collapsing into one rule. A ban on downloadable weights would constrain legitimate deployment and research regardless of how a model was obtained or used. A rule aimed at misappropriation would instead turn on conduct and evidence. The coalition is asking Washington to regulate the transaction that caused harm, not the technical format that made later use possible.

Open weights move operational control to the model user

A hosted model leaves the decisive controls with its provider. The provider chooses the serving infrastructure, usage policy, update schedule, logging regime and price. An open-weight model moves much of that control to the organization running it. A hospital, manufacturer, government agency or security team can keep prompts and outputs on its own systems, adapt the model to a narrow task, inspect its behavior and continue operating if the original developer changes terms.

That distinction is often obscured by the phrase “open source.” Open weights do not necessarily reveal the training data, data-cleaning process, optimizer settings or full training code. The National described the category more precisely: the parameters can be downloaded and adapted even when the development process remains proprietary. The practical product is therefore portable execution, not a complete recipe for reproducing the model.3

Portability changes bargaining power across the stack. Customers can compare cloud providers, purchase their own accelerators, use specialized inference services or run a model inside an existing data boundary. They can also remove restrictions imposed by the original developer. Tom's Hardware noted that OpenAI, Anthropic and Google were absent from the coalition, while the signatories span chips, servers, hosting, enterprise software, model repositories and venture capital. The companies asking for openness generally make money when models can travel between products and infrastructure.4

The coalition turns openness into an industrial policy

The coalition's argument is broader than developer freedom. It presents open weights as a way to keep the American AI market contestable. Nvidia sells the computation used to train and run many models. Microsoft, IBM, Dell and ServiceNow sell infrastructure and enterprise integration. Hugging Face hosts models and development tools. Meta and Mistral publish weights. Investors such as Andreessen Horowitz and Y Combinator benefit when new companies can begin with an existing model instead of negotiating access to a closed platform. Noze's analysis of the signatory list makes the alignment clear: each layer gains when customers can move a model and spend elsewhere in the stack.5

This alignment does not invalidate the argument. It explains why the letter appeared now. Closed-model providers can use safety controls, exclusive capabilities and scale economics to retain customers. Open-weight suppliers answer with local control, lower switching costs and a larger aftermarket for hardware, hosting, fine-tuning and security. The policy dispute is therefore also a dispute over where economic rents accumulate.

The letter also treats openness as a defensive capability. A security team can study a downloadable model, modify it for incident response and test failure modes without waiting for a vendor to approve the use. Independent researchers can reproduce findings against the same weights. Those benefits are real, but they share a cause with the principal danger: the developer can no longer decide who runs the model or what modifications they make.

An open-weight release cannot be recalled

The strongest counterargument begins after publication. A hosted provider can patch a model, suspend an account, block a tool or replace a checkpoint. A released weight file can be copied indefinitely. Researchers Bengüsu Özcan, Alex Petropoulos and Max Reddel argue that advanced open-weight releases are effectively irreversible, that built-in safeguards can be bypassed through fine-tuning or jailbreaking, and that no common standard yet determines when a model is safe enough to release. They propose a tiered system in which the release method depends on demonstrated risk rather than a binary choice between fully closed and fully downloadable.6

That is a harder problem than the coalition's theft framework solves. Misappropriation can be investigated after an actor acquires a model. Irreversible release concerns what every unknown actor may do afterward. Targeted enforcement can punish a company that covertly extracts a competitor's capabilities, but it cannot retrieve weights already mirrored across jurisdictions or restore safeguards removed by a downstream operator.

The credible middle position is therefore conditional openness. Models below defined capability and misuse thresholds can be released broadly. More capable systems may require staged access, independent evaluations, delayed release or restrictions on particular components. The challenge is measurement. Without agreed tests and disclosure rules, “targeted” policy can become selective enforcement, while “open” can become a label applied before risks are understood.

The next policy line will be drawn through enforcement

The immediate test is whether the United States can act against alleged industrial extraction without treating every downloadable model as suspect. Axios reported that the White House is distinguishing legitimate distillation, in which one model helps train another, from what officials describe as industrial-scale covert theft. Sanctions or Entity List action against particular Chinese firms remain possible. That approach would accept the coalition's central distinction while reserving penalties for named conduct and actors.7

Enforcement will reveal whether the distinction is operational or rhetorical. A workable regime needs evidence standards for misappropriation, a definition of abusive extraction, capability tests for unusually dangerous releases and a way to review decisions before restrictions become permanent market barriers. It also needs to avoid converting safety policy into protection for incumbent hosted-model vendors.

This story survives the cut because the coalition has assembled the companies that profit from a portable AI layer and forced a concrete choice onto policymakers. Washington can regulate access to weights, regulate harmful conduct, or construct a tiered release system that does both selectively. The next observable event is a targeted U.S. action against an accused model developer, or a proposal that defines release thresholds across the whole category. Either would show whether officials can separate theft from technique without leaving irreversible risks ungoverned.
