Jodrell Bank Shows How a Flat Science Budget Can Collapse Capability

Britain is ending support for e-MERLIN as a new fibre network comes online, exposing the operating commitments hidden behind capital upgrades and stable headline funding.

7 min read2026-07-28

The cut removes a capability, not just a grant

The Science and Technology Facilities Council said on July 27 that it will end support for e-MERLIN when the current agreement expires in March 2028. The network links seven radio telescopes across England and combines their signals at Jodrell Bank. STFC describes the decision as part of a portfolio reset intended to protect discovery research while making its finances sustainable by 2029-30.1

The immediate consequence is unusually discrete. ITV reported that the University of Manchester must now find another funding arrangement to preserve the Lovell Telescope and e-MERLIN's scientific operations. The site can remain a heritage destination and the headquarters of the Square Kilometre Array Observatory while losing the observing system that makes Jodrell Bank an active national research facility.2

That distinction matters because a research facility is not a portfolio of separable grants. Its value comes from a maintained chain of antennas, fibre, timing, correlators, specialist staff, observing schedules and long scientific records. Remove the operating budget and the chain stops producing capability, even if the physical telescope remains visible. A modest percentage reduction can therefore create an all-or-nothing outcome.1

A flat headline budget can contain a large operational cut

UKRI says STFC's allocation is stable over the next four years, but its forecast cost base is not. Rising costs for complex facilities and services mean the council must reduce net annual operating costs by £162 million relative to forecast by the end of 2029-30. UKRI says no other research council faces an equivalent adjustment.3

The gap emerged through the mechanics of infrastructure rather than a single cancelled appropriation. UKRI's chief executive said energy prices and adverse foreign-exchange movements added more than £50 million a year during the previous spending period, while STFC's core budget rises only from £835 million to £842 million. International subscriptions, electricity and technical operations can therefore consume a nominally flat settlement before a new experiment begins.4

Percentage language obscures this asymmetry. STFC says the particle physics, astronomy and nuclear physics science budget will fall 2.7% over four years. That sounds incremental. For a network with fixed minimum staffing and maintenance requirements, however, the relevant question is whether the remaining allocation crosses the threshold needed to operate at all. Below that floor, the marginal cut does not buy slightly less astronomy. It retires the instrument.1

The portfolio logic also shifts risk onto institutions with the least ability to absorb it. A university can seek grants, donations and contracts, but it cannot easily guarantee decades of utility bills, engineering coverage and replacement parts for a national array. The central funder can diversify those risks across facilities and years. Once support is withdrawn, the host must assemble the same operating certainty from fragmented sources before staff leave and technical knowledge disperses.23

The new fibre network makes the sequencing failure visible

The timing exposes a second problem. In July, e-MERLIN announced completion of an STFC-funded fibre network delivering 100 gigabits per second from each telescope. Together with a new software correlator and planned digitisation systems, it is designed to carry roughly half a terabit per second of observational data to Jodrell Bank. The same month, its public operating support was placed on a path to termination.5

Capital upgrades and operating commitments are often approved through different decisions. One programme pays to increase bandwidth; another later evaluates the annual portfolio and removes the staff and service budget needed to use it. Each decision can look defensible inside its own accounting window. Together they create a stranded public asset whose technical potential has increased just as its institutional runway disappears.5

This does not prove that every upgraded facility deserves permanent funding. It shows that infrastructure appraisal is incomplete when it values construction without pricing the operating envelope, transition path and closure cost. Before approving a major upgrade, the owner should identify the minimum viable annual budget, the expected service life and the conditions that would trigger an orderly exit. Otherwise investment decisions can subsidize capability that the portfolio never commits to operate.3

The same accounting should include option value. A working array can be redirected toward transient events, very-long-baseline observations or new space-monitoring services. A mothballed array is not simply paused: calibration degrades, components age and staff move. Restarting later can cost more than bridging a temporary budget gap. A closure appraisal that records only next year's saving will systematically favor irreversible cuts over reversible reductions in scope.5

Commercial income cannot automatically replace a public-good mission

STFC is applying similar pressure across its facilities. Nature reported that the Diamond Light Source, the Central Laser Facility and the ISIS neutron and muon source face a combined commercial-funding gap of about £42 million a year. The policy asks facilities to obtain more revenue from industry or face reduced operations and possible closure.6

Commercialization can reveal valuable applications and make firms pay for scarce instrument time. It cannot be assumed to finance every capability worth retaining. Long-baseline astronomy produces open research, trains specialists, supports international collaborations and preserves continuous observations whose benefits are dispersed. Those outputs are difficult to sell to one customer even when their national value is real.6

A commercial target can also change what the facility optimizes. Industrial users generally pay for predictable access, confidential results and near-term deliverables. Astronomy often requires open allocation by scientific merit, rapid response to rare events and patient observation whose payoff is uncertain. Revenue can support the shared platform, but it should not silently displace the public mission that justified the instrument in the first place.6

Jodrell Bank does have adjacent markets, including space-domain awareness. Its telescopes recently helped demonstrate radar observations of satellites and debris. That is a credible route to co-funding, not proof that a national astronomy network can immediately replace a £2.8 million annual public allocation. Times Higher Education reported that the support is being removed while alternative funding remains prospective, leaving the facility to solve the revenue problem after the decision rather than before it.57

A credible transition needs a capability ledger

STFC's strongest case is that it cannot preserve every existing programme while also funding the next generation. Its July statement says the portfolio was chosen to protect postdoctoral researchers, PhD opportunities and international leadership. ITV reported that STFC is prioritizing newer radio-astronomy facilities and gives Manchester until March 2028 to pursue alternatives.12

The missing machine-checkable object is a capability ledger. For each facility, government should publish the minimum operating floor, users served, replacement capability, sunk upgrades, shutdown and restart costs, data-series consequences, and credible external revenue already contracted. Parliament's science committee has already recorded that multidisciplinary-facility funding will fall 15% over four years while the PPAN research budget falls 2.7%. Those portfolio figures do not show which national capabilities disappear at the threshold.8

The ledger should also separate evidence from advocacy. Researchers will naturally defend their instruments, while funders will emphasize portfolio sustainability. A common template would force both sides to disclose assumptions: expected users, contracted income, replacement timelines and the consequences of delay. That does not remove political judgment. It makes the judgment auditable and prevents a flat budget from being presented as stable capability.48

For e-MERLIN, the next evidence should be concrete. It needs a transition plan from STFC and Manchester, priced commitments from government, industry or philanthropy, and an explanation of how the new fibre and correlator investment will be used after March 2028. If no viable operator emerges, the closure decision should include the cost of preserving data, specialist knowledge and restart options. The policy failure is not choosing between old and new science. It is making that choice without showing what capability the country is actually surrendering.28
