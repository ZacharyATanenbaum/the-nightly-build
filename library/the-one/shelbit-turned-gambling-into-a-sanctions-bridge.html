Shelbit Turned Iran’s Gambling Black Market Into a Sanctions Bridge

The unlicensed Dubai exchange connected more than 2,000 Iranian betting sites and sanctioned institutions to global crypto liquidity, exposing an enforcement system that identified entities after much of the money had moved.

8 min read2026-08-01

A hidden exchange moved at least $4 billion

A Dubai company with no usable public website and an office hidden behind a watch-trading sign processed at least $4 billion in cryptocurrency since May 2024. Reuters identified Shelbit as the hub connecting more than 2,000 Farsi-language gambling sites, Iran’s central bank, sanctioned Iranian entities, bitcoin mining and major global crypto venues.1

The network matters because each piece looked ordinary when viewed alone. Iranian gamblers paid through domestic cards and bank transfers. Website operators converted balances into crypto. Shelbit pooled the flows in Dubai. Global exchanges supplied the liquid market where tokens could be traded, transferred or converted again. The chain crossed legal jurisdictions without requiring any one institution to see the entire transaction.

Dubai’s Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority had already ordered Shelbit to stop unlicensed activity in January 2025. On July 24, 2026, the regulator issued a new notice saying the company continued to provide exchange services without a license, onboard customers without mandatory identity checks and market itself without authorization. VARA imposed another fine and ordered an immediate halt.2

The new evidence does not prove that the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps directly controlled Shelbit or every gambling site. Reuters could not establish that ownership or determine where much of the money ultimately went. It does show a working financial bridge between a prohibited domestic market, state-controlled payment infrastructure and international crypto liquidity. The failure was not that the money was invisible. It was that legal status, ownership and risk information reached the institutions able to stop it after large volumes had already passed through.

The first gateway was Iran’s own payment system

Cryptocurrency entered the chain only after the gambling network collected rials. Reuters found that the sites accepted Iran’s online payment system, which is closely overseen by the central bank. Former officials told the news organization that large-scale online gambling required access to domestic bank cards and transfers, and that security institutions moved to supervise financial enterprises that became large enough to operate independently.1

This changes the usual account of sanctions evasion. The hard problem is not converting a token into another token. It is joining two financial systems that apply different identity, licensing and political controls. The domestic rail identifies Iranian customers and moves local currency. The offshore exchange turns those balances into assets that can cross borders without correspondent banks. A global venue then provides depth and counterparties.

U.S. sanctions actions describe the same architecture at a larger scale. On June 2, the Treasury Department designated Nobitex, Wallex, Bitpin and Ramzinex, saying the four Iranian exchanges helped regime insiders reach international digital-asset markets. Treasury said Nobitex handled more than half of Iranian crypto inflows in 2025 and processed transactions linked to the IRGC, while Ramzinex had processed more than $2.45 billion.3

Associated Press reporting on that action captured why the exchange layer matters. Domestic firms give sanctioned users a place to acquire digital assets, but the value of those assets depends on access to foreign markets and issuers. Secondary sanctions are meant to make international firms treat the connection itself as costly, even when a transaction does not name an Iranian government agency on its face.4

Shelbit converted a black market into global liquidity

Blockchain records reviewed by Reuters showed at least $676 million moving from Shelbit-linked addresses to Binance after May 2024. About $540 million moved after Dubai regulators fined Shelbit in 2025. An independent investigator said he warned Binance about Shelbit’s Iranian ties in October of that year, yet additional transfers followed.1

Binance said Shelbit never held an account and was not sanctioned. It said users associated with Shelbit interacted with the platform, that its compliance program investigated those users, froze relevant accounts and reported them to law enforcement. It also said an independent analytics provider had not classified the flows as high risk. Those statements expose the central operational problem. A regulator can name a company, while an exchange sees customer accounts and wallet addresses that may be connected to it only through later analysis.

Chainalysis’s review of the June sanctions illustrates how much depends on entity resolution. The firm had to label addresses for four Iranian exchanges and their executives so customers could screen exposure. It estimated that the Iranian crypto ecosystem received $7.78 billion in 2025 and that IRGC-associated addresses accounted for more than half of value received in the fourth quarter. The relevant control is therefore a continuously updated map between legal entities, people, accounts and wallets, not a static country filter.5

That map is inherently incomplete. Wallets can be created quickly, transactions can pass through intermediaries and a service may never appear as the named customer of the final exchange. The practical question is how much indirect exposure should trigger review, delay or rejection. A rule that blocks every remote connection would produce large numbers of false positives. A rule that waits for a formal designation gives a network time to move.

The notices attached to companies after the transfers

The timeline shows three separate clocks. Shelbit appears to have started operating in May 2024. Dubai regulators acted against its unlicensed business in January 2025. The large transfers to global venues continued, and VARA’s July 2026 notice added failures in customer identification and cross-border financial-integrity concerns.2

National investigations moved on another schedule. The U.S. Justice Department began examining alleged Iranian use of Binance for sanctions evasion and transfers to IRGC-linked groups months before the Shelbit investigation was published. Euronews reported that investigators sought company documents, transaction records and interviews from people familiar with the flows.6

Sanctions designations moved on a third schedule. Treasury’s April action against 35 shadow-banking facilitators described networks of exchange houses, front companies and overseas accounts moving the equivalent of tens of billions of dollars for Iran’s armed forces. Those designations can freeze property within U.S. reach and warn foreign institutions, but they are necessarily built from evidence about structures that already exist.7

None of these controls is useless. Together they produced the records that now expose the network. The weakness is synchronization. A local licensing violation, an analytics warning, a foreign criminal inquiry and a sanctions designation do not automatically become one machine-readable risk decision at every exchange. The network profits from the interval between recognition and propagation.

Blockchain tracing is the strongest defense of the system

The strongest counterargument is visible in the investigation itself. Reuters and specialist firms reconstructed years of transfers across wallets. VARA escalated its action. Binance says it froze accounts. Treasury and analytics companies have published addresses that exchanges and stablecoin issuers can screen. In July, Chainalysis reported that four wallets added to the Central Bank of Iran’s sanctions record had received $165 million in stablecoins and that Tether immediately froze $131 million still held in them.8

Traditional shadow banking can hide beneficial ownership behind invoices, shell companies and correspondent accounts that investigators cannot inspect directly. Public blockchains preserve a transaction history. Once an address is attributed, earlier and later counterparties can be examined. That persistence raises the chance of later seizure, designation or prosecution.

Traceability, however, is not the same as prevention. It works best when investigators can assign an address before assets leave a controllable account or token. The Central Bank freeze succeeded because the issuer could immobilize stablecoins still sitting in identified wallets. Bitcoin already transferred through intermediaries or assets withdrawn to a venue outside cooperating jurisdictions present a different problem.

The evidence therefore supports a narrower conclusion than “crypto defeats sanctions.” Crypto creates a fast international settlement layer whose records can be unusually transparent. Enforcement succeeds when identity attribution, legal authority and control over the asset converge before settlement becomes irreversible. Shelbit operated in the gap between those three conditions.

The next control belongs at the exchange boundary

This story survived the cut because it identifies a specific sanctions boundary. Governments can outlaw domestic activity, designate companies and publish wallet addresses. Those actions matter only when the exchange or issuer at the next liquid market can connect its customers and counterparties to the named network quickly enough to act.

The next observable evidence should be concrete. VARA can disclose the scope and timing of Shelbit’s continued activity after the 2025 order. Binance can explain when associated accounts were detected, what funds were frozen and why the transfers were initially rated below its high-risk threshold. U.S. authorities can designate identified wallets or bring charges that establish which people controlled them. Each record would reduce the gap between a corporate name and the transaction graph.

The policy response should target that gap rather than demand impossible certainty. Exchanges need a documented threshold for indirect exposure to an unlicensed or sanctioned service, a time limit for escalating credible external warnings and a record of what happened to funds after review. Regulators need a channel that turns licensing and sanctions actions into address and account intelligence across jurisdictions. Analytics providers need to preserve uncertainty instead of presenting a probabilistic cluster as proven ownership.

Shelbit did not need to defeat every sanction. It needed domestic access, an offshore converter and enough time before the next institution recognized the connection. The $4 billion estimate is evidence of how long that interval can remain economically useful. The next enforcement record will show whether authorities are closing the interval or merely naming another network after it has finished moving.
