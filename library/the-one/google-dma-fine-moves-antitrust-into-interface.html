Google's €890 Million Fine Moves Antitrust Into the Interface

Europe's latest DMA decisions judge Search prominence and Play Store fees by the choices they preserve in practice, turning product architecture into the compliance test.

8 min read2026-07-26

Europe penalizes the choices built into Search and Play

The European Commission fined Google €890 million on July 23 after finding two separate breaches of the Digital Markets Act. The first decision assigns €460 million to the way Google places its own shopping, hotel, transport, and sports services inside Search. The second assigns €430 million to Play Store rules that limit how app developers direct users toward purchases elsewhere. Google has been ordered to end both practices.1

The sum is large enough to attract attention and small enough to miss the important part. The Commission is judging the architecture through which a dominant platform presents choices. Search can return rival links while still making Google's own service more visible. Play can let a developer mention an outside offer while preserving fees and restrictions that make the route uneconomic. The DMA treats those outcomes as non-compliance even when a formal permission exists.

Google says the remedy damages useful products. Its public response argues that compliance removes live prices and availability from European Search and weakens protections against malicious payment links on Android.2 The dispute is therefore no longer confined to contract language. It turns on whether regulators can require genuine choice without forcing a platform to discard integration that users value.

Permission means little when the platform keeps the toll

The Play decision exposes the difference between access and an economically usable route. The Commission accepts that Google may charge for helping a developer acquire a customer. It objects to the level of steering-related fees and the length of time they continue after the user leaves Play. A developer can technically send a customer to a website and still owe enough to Google that the alternative changes little.

That is a systems problem disguised as a pricing dispute. A platform controls the discovery channel, the account relationship, the warning screens, the billing defaults, and the fees attached to each path. Changing one screen or adding one link does not create competition if the rest of the transaction keeps the same economics. Reuters reported that the Commission viewed Google's revised Play terms as progress, while continuing to assess them against the cease-and-desist order.3

The relevant test is the whole path from discovery to payment. Developers need to tell users about another price, link to it without punitive friction, complete the sale, and retain enough of the revenue for the outside channel to matter. The Verge noted that Google has 60 days to change both Search and Play, with periodic penalties available if the new design fails the order.4 Compliance will be measured by the route that remains, not the label attached to it.

Search ranking is product geometry with economic consequences

Search self-preferencing is harder to isolate because ranking and presentation are the product. Google does more than order blue links. It builds hotel cards, flight boxes, maps, prices, filters, and answer panels. Those modules can be faster and more informative than a list of outside pages. They also let the company running the general search engine occupy the best visual space with its own specialized services.

Google has spent years testing layouts meant to satisfy the DMA. In a 2024 compliance update, the company described more than 20 changes, including dedicated units for comparison sites and the removal of features such as flight information and clickable map functions. It also said direct booking clicks to some airlines, hotels, and retailers fell after those changes.5 That claim comes from Google and serves its policy position, but it identifies a real tradeoff: helping comparison intermediaries can divert traffic from both Google and the underlying supplier.

Independent evidence shows that small interface changes can redirect behavior. A 2026 Marketing Science study examined Google's removal of clickable map features in Europe and compared the resulting traffic with markets outside the DMA change. The research found that altering a prominent path changed how users reached mapping services.6 The policy instrument is therefore not symbolic. A link's placement, visual treatment, and number of required clicks can redistribute demand across an ecosystem.

Google's strongest defense is product degradation

Google's argument is strongest when it stops sounding like a demand for immunity and starts describing the user cost of enforced separation. A search engine that combines inventory, location, price, and availability can answer a query faster than a neutral page that sends the user through several intermediaries. An app store that warns about external payment can reduce fraud and simplify refunds. Some integration is useful precisely because one operator controls the stack.

The Commission's task is to distinguish useful integration from a design that protects an adjacent business. The Guardian reported that Google can appeal and seek interim measures, while critics of the company called the fine too small relative to its revenue.7 Neither side resolves the design question. A larger fine does not specify a better Search layout. A security warning can be justified while its wording, timing, or repetition still deters lawful alternatives.

A credible remedy needs behavioral evidence. Regulators should compare click-through rates, conversion, fees, latency, fraud, and supplier traffic before and after each change. Google should be allowed to preserve features that produce measurable user value, provided rival services receive equivalent opportunities under transparent criteria. The hard part is not declaring a principle. It is defining tests that cannot be satisfied by cosmetic changes or gamed by forcing the incumbent to make every option equally bad.

The regulator still has to define a stable endpoint

The Commission's announcement describes obligations but does not publish a permanent user interface. Google is already testing new presentation formats, and regulators have called that work substantial progress. Reuters also reported that the same principles may be applied to AI Overviews and AI Mode.3 That extension matters because generated answers compress search, comparison, and recommendation into a single surface. The old distinction between a general result and Google's own specialized service becomes harder to draw.

The enforcement model must remain stable across product changes without freezing the product. A rule tied to one widget will expire when the widget disappears. A rule tied only to broad fairness language risks becoming negotiation without an endpoint. The practical answer is a small set of machine-observable tests: equivalent access to ranking inputs, comparable visual treatment, disclosed fees, measurable switching friction, and auditable outcomes for rivals and users.

Europe also has to defend that regime against external pressure. The Associated Press reported on July 24 that the United States would open a trade investigation into EU fines on American technology companies, with tariffs raised as a possible response.8 The conflict gives Google another route to challenge the decisions, but it does not answer whether the platform's current choices are contestable.

Sixty days will show whether the DMA can govern systems

This story survived the cut because the decisions move digital competition law from a prohibition on paper into the design of two products used by billions of people. The fine records past non-compliance. The next 60 days will reveal whether the Commission can specify a workable target and whether Google can meet it without reducing Search and Play to weaker versions of themselves.1

The observable events are concrete. Google can publish revised Search layouts and Play fee terms, seek interim relief, or appeal. The Commission can accept the changes, impose periodic penalties, or extend the decisions to AI-generated search surfaces. The useful result would be neither a protected incumbent nor an interface designed by committee. It would be a platform where integration earns its place through user value and rivals can reach customers without crossing a toll road controlled by their competitor.
