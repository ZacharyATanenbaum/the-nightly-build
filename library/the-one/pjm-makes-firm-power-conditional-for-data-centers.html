PJM Makes Firm Power Conditional for New Data Centers

America’s largest grid will buy 15 years of backup capacity while placing new loads without matching supply on a service that can reduce them before ordinary emergency customers.

9 min read2026-07-29

A grid connection no longer guarantees firm service

PJM Interconnection’s board decided on July 27 that new large electricity users will no longer receive the same reliability bargain by default. A new customer that wants ordinary firm service will need to bring or contract for enough new capacity to cover its demand. If it does not, the distributor serving it must place the load on an Interim Resource Adequacy Service that can require reductions before PJM calls on conventional pre-emergency demand response.1

The rule is aimed principally at data centers. PJM serves 67 million people across 13 states and the District of Columbia. Its board projects roughly 70 gigawatts of new large-load demand by 2038, after about 15 gigawatts of generation retired in the region since 2022. The board said two consecutive capacity auctions have already procured less than the system’s reliability requirement.12

This creates a distinction that power planning usually hides. Physical connection, energy consumption and firm reliability are different products. A site may be wired to the grid and buy electricity in normal hours without holding an unconditional claim on scarce capacity during system stress. PJM’s decision turns that distinction into an operating rule for the largest new loads.1

The capacity auction hit its price cap and still came up short

PJM’s capacity auction pays generators and demand-side resources to be available in a future delivery year. It is supposed to procure enough dependable capacity for the system’s projected peak plus a reserve margin. The July auction reached its temporary price ceiling of $325 per megawatt-day and still cleared about 6.8 gigawatts below the reliability requirement.3

That combination matters more than either result alone. A high auction price normally attracts more supply or suppresses demand. Once a binding cap is reached, price can no longer perform that balancing function. Reuters reported that capacity prices have risen more than tenfold since 2024. Without the temporary cap, PJM calculated that much of the region would have cleared at $554.72 per megawatt-day, with the ComEd area above $775.3

A short auction does not mean that 6.8 gigawatts will vanish from the grid tomorrow. It means the forward system has less committed reserve than PJM’s reliability standard calls for. The shortage also reflects retired plants, delayed interconnections and a market that has not financed replacement capacity quickly enough. Data-center growth is the largest new demand shock, but it landed on a supply process that was already slow.13

The backstop buys 15 years of capacity outside the normal auction

PJM will now procure outside that normal auction. Its Reliability Backstop Procurement has two paths: facilitated bilateral contracts between large loads and resource developers, and a central purchase that acts as a safety net. The initial central target equals the 2028–29 auction shortfall, reduced for documented contracts, self-supply and qualifying demand response so PJM does not buy the same capacity twice.4

The central procurement offers what the annual market could not: a revenue horizon long enough to finance construction. Selected resources can receive a capacity-only commitment lasting through the 2042–43 delivery year. Offers are pay-as-bid and settled as contracts for differences against future capacity-auction revenue. PJM set the maximum volume-weighted willingness to pay at $555 per megawatt-day. Bids are due from September 30 through October 21, with results scheduled by December 2.4

Fifteen years transfers risk in both directions. Developers gain predictable revenue and can finance projects that an annual auction price cannot support. Customers inherit the cost if forecasts weaken, projects arrive after the shortage has eased, or the procurement locks in an expensive technology mix. The board added delay charges and will reduce the target for private contracts, but the commitment still converts an uncertain demand forecast into a long-lived obligation.45

Firm service now requires matching supply

The second half of the decision changes service rather than procurement. PJM will create a registry for loads of at least 50 megawatts at a single site or closely grouped sites. Beginning with the 2027–28 delivery year, distributors must identify new large loads that lack sufficient “bring your own new capacity” supply or backstop capacity.6

Those loads can still connect. Their service becomes conditional. When available generation is insufficient, PJM may assign a reduction quantity to a zone, and the transmission owner or distributor must reduce the designated load within 10 minutes. The new action sits ahead of pre-emergency load management in PJM’s emergency sequence. A data center may avoid interruption by switching to backup resources, provided its withdrawal disappears from the PJM system.6

This is a two-tier reliability bargain. A new large load that provides matching accredited capacity receives the same firm treatment as existing demand. A load that relies on the common pool accepts earlier curtailment. The rule is less blunt than banning connections until generation is built, and more enforceable than a voluntary promise to procure supply later. It also makes the site’s operational flexibility economically valuable: backup generation, batteries or interruptible computing can substitute for some grid capacity if they perform when called.16

Cost allocation stops at the state line

PJM can define the wholesale obligation, but it cannot decide which retail customer pays it. The backstop’s costs first flow to zones and load-serving entities. Electric distributors are expected to assign those obligations to customers under new retail frameworks. If they do not, PJM’s fallback allocates the cost across all load in the affected area, including customers that are not large loads.4

That is the unresolved part of the bargain. The federal government’s Ratepayer Protection Pledge now has 23 governors and at least 187 companies as signatories, according to the Associated Press. The pledge is voluntary. PJM’s board said state action is essential because the grid operator lacks jurisdiction to allocate retail costs directly to individual data centers.71

The registry gives regulators a factual base they previously lacked: location, expected demand, ramp schedule, contracted capacity and backup capability. It does not write a tariff. States and utilities must still determine whether a data center pays through an individual contract, a dedicated rate class, a capacity obligation or a broader zonal charge. Until those rules exist, the customer most responsible for the investment and the customer legally billed for it can remain different entities.61

Forecast risk is the strongest objection

The strongest objection is not that large loads should receive free reliability. It is that PJM may socialize forecast error while claiming to prevent cost shifting. Data-center projects are announced faster than they are completed, and the 70-gigawatt projection includes demand that may be delayed, relocated or cancelled. A central purchase tied to that forecast could overbuild and leave ordinary customers paying for unused insurance.14

Stakeholders had advanced a subscription-based backstop with unusually broad support. It relied more heavily on utilities and large loads electing how much capacity PJM should buy. The board rejected that structure because voluntary subscriptions might not cover the full reliability shortfall. Its alternative gives procurement certainty at the cost of more centralized forecast risk.15

There is also a sequencing problem. New resources still use PJM’s standard interconnection process. A 15-year contract can make a project financeable, but it cannot make network upgrades appear sooner. Canary Media reported that developers see long-term contracts as necessary because generators need revenue certainty, while bilateral deals can place more risk directly on investors and large loads. The central backstop is therefore best understood as last-resort insurance, not proof that PJM has repaired its generation queue or capacity market.48

FERC will test whether the bargain can hold

This decision survived the cut because it establishes a concrete rule for an abstract argument about AI infrastructure. Computing demand is no longer treated only as another forecast input. Its claim on firm grid reliability becomes conditional on supply, flexibility or payment for a backstop.16

The design can still fail. FERC must accept the wholesale tariff changes. States must create retail cost-allocation rules that prevent the fallback charge from spreading to ordinary customers. Developers must bid projects that can actually clear interconnection and deliver by 2032. Data centers must prove that early curtailment is operationally real rather than a contractual label.146

The next evidence arrives quickly. PJM intends to file at FERC by the end of July, begin the bid window on September 30 and publish selections by December 2. Those filings and bids will show whether the new bargain moves risk to the loads causing the shortage or merely gives the same regional shortfall a longer contract.14
